DLR, Institute of Flight Systems · morphAIR group · Braunschweig · Mar to Jul 2026

Reduced-order aerodynamic model of the PROTEUS morphing wing

Problem
PROTEUS is a jet-powered unmanned research aircraft whose trailing edge is morphed by ten actuators per half-wing. The first aerodynamic model used a vortex-lattice method and a genetic algorithm, which is too costly for real-time flight simulation or reinforcement-learning training.
What I did
  • Owned the aerodynamics block of the team's simulation chain; other team members handled the other blocks and the integration of all of them.
  • Developed a multi-fidelity methodology: a low-fidelity model with a gradient-based optimizer finds minimum-drag morphing configurations across the flight envelope, including manoeuvres with a prescribed rolling moment.
  • Re-analysed those geometries with a higher-fidelity model of the complete aircraft and condensed them into a lookup-table model, with local actuator effects recovered through superposed increments.
  • Compared lookup tables, VLM, lifting-line theory and surrogate models; evaluated AVL, VSPAERO and XFLR5 for the FMU-based simulation environment (PyFDM).
Result
Delivered the model and a report on the proposed tool. In the low-fidelity model the optimal configurations reduce drag by 1.5% to 3.2% relative to the undeformed wing. The lookup table reproduces the vortex-lattice reference exactly for the reference geometries and stays within 12% under combined inputs, where superposition neglects cross-coupling. A paper derived from this work, written with L. Kracke (DLR), is not yet published.

UTN Haedo + TU Braunschweig · Thesis work · 2025 to 2026

Lifting-line theory for morphing-wing drag optimisation

Problem
Morphing wings need design tools that are cheap enough for early design stages. Full 3D CFD or aero-structural coupling is too expensive to explore many spanwise camber distributions.
What I did
  • Coupled 2D CFD sectional data (lift, drag and moment of the baseline and morphed airfoils) with a classical lifting-line solver and a parametric trailing-edge morphing model.
  • Formulated a constrained drag minimisation at constant lift, with the angle of attack and the deflection at 50 spanwise stations as design variables, solved with SLSQP within the mechanism's deformation limits.
  • Compared the optimised wing against the baseline for lift coefficients from 0.3 to 1.6, in cruise and high-lift conditions.
Result
Consistent drag reduction, much larger off-design than in cruise: +4.9% in CL/CD at CL = 0.4 and +48.9% at CL = 1.2. The optimised wing also shows a smoother spanwise drag distribution and a steeper lift gradient toward the tip, which favours root-first stall. The CFD underpredicts absolute drag (about 30%), so the results are used comparatively. Published as the preprint "Lifting Line Theory for Morphing Wing Applications" (submitted to CEAS Aeronautical Journal); code available on request.

Laboratory for Aerodynamics & Fluid Mechanics, UTN Haedo · 2023 to 2026

CFD and wind-tunnel study of deformable airfoils

Problem
Start exploring the adaptive trailing-edge concept in the laboratory and validate experimentally that it delays flow separation.
What I did
  • Pre-screened trailing-edge deflections of a NACA 4415 with XFOIL, using an analytical camber law that adds a smooth trailing-edge deflection to the baseline airfoil.
  • High-fidelity 2D CFD in OpenFOAM (RANS, k-ω SST with the γ-Reθ transition model, C-mesh of about 500,000 cells, y+ near 1) of the baseline and the morphed airfoil. Moved on from Spalart-Allmaras and plain k-ω SST after their drag overestimation (about 50% and 30%).
  • Validated the baseline against experimental airfoil data, then compared it with the morphed airfoil at 0° and 5° angle of attack (course project, Nov 2024).
  • Qualitative wind-tunnel tests with oilflow (28.4 m/s, 15 °C) and smoke visualisation; the smoke runs compared a flapped airfoil with the morphing one at 0°, 5° and 10° angle of attack.
  • Maintained the Hele-Shaw cell experimental equipment.
Result
In the CFD, the morphed airfoil raises Cl by 0.16 to 0.2 for about 10% more drag, so efficiency improves: Cl/Cd +27% and Cl1.5/Cd +50% at 0°, and +7% and +17% at 5°. The baseline matched the reference data within 6.8% in Cl at 0° and 1.7% at 5°; drag was less accurate (23% and 10%), so the comparison is relative. At this Reynolds number the flow was unsteady, so the natural next step is URANS. In the wind-tunnel tests, the morphing airfoil showed the expected delay in flow separation. The model was not sealed against the tunnel wall, so air leaked through and created vortex effects: the results are qualitative, and a sealed setup is needed for a cleaner validation.

TU Braunschweig, Institute of Fluid Mechanics · Research report, July 2026

Impingement limit of small droplets on rough surfaces

Problem
In-flight icing starts with supercooled droplets freezing into small beads that later grow into ice feathers. Icing codes treat this roughness only as a turbulence boundary condition. Does a single bead, placed at the impingement limit of the droplet size that formed it, change the water catch only locally, or does it shift the impingement limits themselves?
What I did
  • Computed the flow around a NACA 0012 airfoil in OpenFOAM (RANS, k-ω SST), after a grid and domain convergence study.
  • Integrated droplet trajectories in a separate post-processing step (Lagrangian equation of motion, adaptive Runge-Kutta 4(5)) and derived the collection efficiency.
  • Validated the workflow against the experimental collection-efficiency data of Papadakis et al. and the NASA LEWICE code.
  • Modelled beads of 40, 60 and 80 µm as semicircular ridges at the corresponding impingement limits and recomputed the collection efficiency for a range of droplet diameters.
Result
A bead produces a sharp, local peak in collection efficiency at its own position, up to six times the smooth-surface value, while the distribution and the impingement limits elsewhere stay indistinguishable from the smooth airfoil. Whether a droplet hits the bead is set by the droplet's own impingement limit (a leading-edge Stokes number), and every icing-relevant droplet is too inertial to be deflected around the bead. So the water-catch effect of early roughness can be predicted from smooth-wall data alone, and the result suggests why roughness grows preferentially near the impingement limits, where feathers start. The model is 2D with a single bead and an all-stick impact assumption, which the report discusses as limitations.
